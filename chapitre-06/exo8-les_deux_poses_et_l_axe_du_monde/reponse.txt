When the two methods agree:
Both methods agree perfectly when the head is in the neutral pose (neutral),
looking straight ahead towards -Z with zero rotation. In this upright
orientation, the head's local right vector coincides exactly with the world's
X axis (1, 0, 0). Consequently, both methods yield identical eye positions:
L (0.2685, 1.7000, 1.2000) and R (0.3315, 1.7000, 1.2000), with a measured gap
of exactly 0.0 mm. This perfect match while looking straight ahead is dangerous
because it can easily lead a developer to believe the calculation is correct.

When they diverge:
The two methods diverge as soon as the head rotates. For roll 30 (head tilted
30 degrees sideways), the interpupillary axis tilts in the XY plane: the left
eye drops to y = 1.6842 m and shifts to x = 0.2727 m, whereas the world-axis
method leaves y at 1.7000 m. The spatial gap between the two left eye positions
is 16.3 mm. For yaw 90 (head turned 90 degrees), the divergence is massive: the
head's right vector rotates to align with the room's Z axis, placing the eyes
at z = 1.2315 m and z = 1.1685 m. The world-axis method still offsets along X,
producing a discrepancy of 44.5 mm (sqrt(2) * 31.5 mm), nearly the entire IPD.

What a visitor would feel:
A visitor wearing a headset with eyes locked to the world's X axis would suffer
from severe visual confusion, eye strain, and motion sickness. Human stereo
depth perception relies on retinal disparity perpendicular to the line of
sight. At yaw 90, offsetting along the world X axis puts the disparity along
the visual axis or creates vertical disparity when tilting: the brain cannot
fuse the two retinal images into a coherent 3D scene, producing double vision
(diplopia) and the impression that the world shears and squashes unnaturally
whenever the head moves.
