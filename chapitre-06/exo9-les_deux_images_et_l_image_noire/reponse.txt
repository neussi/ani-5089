What differs most:
The regions that differ most are located along the vertical silhouettes and
edges of objects located close to the observer, particularly the edges of the
wooden table and its legs, the frame of the window, and the door opening. In
difference.png, these edges stand out as bright white and grey contours against
the dark backdrop, reaching channel differences up to 94 (scaled by four to
saturate at 255). A total of 78421 pixels out of 460800 show a gap greater
than 1 between the left and right eyes.

Why there:
Horizontal binocular disparity is inversely proportional to depth: nearby
objects exhibit a much larger angular shift between the two vantage points than
distant walls. At the boundary of an object, this horizontal shift reveals
regions that are visible to one eye but occluded to the other (monocular
occlusion). A pixel that samples the dark wood of a table leg in the left eye
samples the bright floor or wall in the right eye, yielding a huge color gap.
Conversely, large uniform surfaces show little difference even when shifted,
because a uniform color shifted onto itself produces identical values. The
difference image thus acts directly as an optical depth and occlusion map.

What the black proves:
The pitch-black image (difference-vue-oubliee.png, with 0 differing pixels out
of 460800 and a max gap of 0) proves that when both cameras share the same
position, the two rendering pipelines execute strictly deterministically,
yielding identical outputs. This proves conclusively that the 78421 differing
pixels in difference.png stem entirely from the 63 mm interpupillary offset,
and not from animation noise, shader jitter, or timestamp discrepancies.
However, black alone does not prove the images are correct: two blank white
targets also yield a black subtraction. It is meaningful only because both
individual eye images are properly rendered views of the 3D room.
