Where the off-centring lives:
The off-centring lives in the third column of the projection matrix, in
coefficients mat[2][0] and mat[2][1] (column 2, rows 0 and 1). In the clip-space
transform, they multiply the view-space depth z: x_clip = x * mat[0][0] +
z * mat[2][0], and y_clip = y * mat[1][1] + z * mat[2][1]. After division by
w_clip = -z, these terms become constant translation offsets in Normalized
Device Coordinates: -mat[2][0] and -mat[2][1]. They shift the optical centre
(principal point) across the image to align with the headset lens and the
wearer's pupil, without introducing perspective keystoning or distortion.

What these terms are worth when symmetric:
When the field of view is symmetric, the left angle equals minus the right
angle (angleLeft = -angleRight) and the bottom angle equals minus the top angle
(angleDown = -angleUp). Because the tangent function is odd, tanLeft = -tanRight
and tanDown = -tanUp. The numerators tanRight + tanLeft and tanUp + tanDown are
both identically 0.0. The off-centring coefficients evaluate to exactly
mat[2][0] = 0.000000 and mat[2][1] = 0.000000. The optical centre is therefore
placed squarely at the geometric centre (0, 0) of the viewport.

What else I checked:
I checked the asymmetrical configuration of the Quest 2 left eye (left -52 deg,
right 45 deg, up 48 deg, down -50 deg) by comparing my hand-written matrix
against the engine's NkCamera3D using SetNearFar(0.05, 100) and SetFovAsym.
Here, the horizontal off-centring term mat[2][0] evaluates to approximately
-0.1228 and the vertical term mat[2][1] evaluates to approximately -0.0352.
Comparing all sixteen coefficients against NkCamera3D::GetProj() confirmed an
exact match across all four rows, with a largest gap of 0.000000, identical to
the float precision of the engine.
